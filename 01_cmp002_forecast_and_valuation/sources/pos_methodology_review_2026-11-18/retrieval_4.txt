L108: The proper interpretation of drug development programs from clinical trial data requires some understanding of the drug development process, especially in cases of missing data. This is particularly important for estimating a drug candidate’s POS【37†Image: Inline graphic†cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov】, which is typically estimated by multiplying the empirical POS of Phase 1 (safety), 2 (efficacy for a given indication), and 3 (efficacy for larger populations and against alternatives) trials.
L109: If, for example, Phase 2 data are missing for certain approved drugs, the estimated POS【38†Image: Inline graphic†cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov】 would be biased downward. Here, we take a different approach to estimating POSs.
L110: Consider an idealized process in which every drug development program passes through Phase 1, 2, and 3 trials, in this order. This is plausible, since each of these stages involves distinct predefined tests, all of which are required by regulators in any new drug application (NDA). If we observe data for Phases 1 and 3 but not Phase 2 for a given drug-indication pair, our idealized process implies that there was at least one Phase 2 trial that occurred, but is missing from our data set.
L111: Accordingly, we impute the successful completion of Phase 2 in these cases. There exist some cases where Phase 2 trials are skipped, as with the recent example of Aducanumab (BIIB037), Biogen’s Alzheimer’s candidate, as reported by 【39†Root (2014)】. Since skipping Phase 2 trials is motivated by compelling Phase 1 data, imputing the successful completion of Phase 2 trials in these cases to trace drug development paths may not be a bad approximation.
L112: In addition, we make the standard assumption that Phase 1/2 and Phase 2/3 trials are to be considered as Phase 2 and Phase 3, respectively.
L113: These assumptions allow us to more accurately reconstruct ‘drug development paths’ for individual drug-indication pairs, which in turn yield more accurate POS estimates.

L804@P18: Part 2. Predictive Analysis of Clinical Success
L805@P18: In Part 1, we estimated success rates using the drug-indication pathways gathered from the Biomedtracker
L806@P18: database. We can now incorporate additional information, called “features,” for each drug development
L807@P18: program using machine learning methods to produce forward-looking measures—forecasts—of the outcome
L808@P18: of ongoing programs. This type of analysis provides more timely and relevant estimates than standalone
L809@P18: analyses of historical success rates.
L810@P18: Some examples of features included in this predictive analysis are the characteristics of the drug, its indication,
L811@P18: its sponsor, and its clinical trial design. There are more than 200 features used in the QLS forecasts, a sample
L812@P18: of which can be found in Figure 12. In much the same way that linear regression models employ regressors,
L813@P18: or “right-hand-side” variables, to predict the “left-hand-side” or the dependent variable, these features
L814@P18: contain useful signals about drug development that allow the outcomes of drug development programs to
L815@P18: be more accurately predicted than before.
L816@P18: Sample of the 200-plus features driving success rate probabilities
L817@P18: Figure 12: Sample of the 200-plus features extracted from Pharmaprojects, Trialtrove, and Biomedtracker.
L818@P18: The QLS approach uses state of the art machine-learning techniques to analyze clinical trial data. While the
L819@P18: details of the QLS model are proprietary, an example of a commonly used machine-learning algorithm is a
L820@P18: random forest classifier. The random forest classifier is an algorithm that takes the average prediction from
L821@P18: a multitude of yes-no decision trees (i.e., a “forest”) that are applied to the features (see Figure 13).
L822@P18: When training a decision tree, one task is to learn the most informative questions to ask at each branch
L823@P18: point of the tree. For example, the algorithm might ask, “Is the drug sponsor a big pharma company?” If the
L824@P18: answer is “yes,” then the algorithm would move to the “yes” branch of the decision tree and then ask, “Is
L825@P18: the trial double-blind?” On the other hand, if the answer is “no,” then the algorithm would move to the “no”
L826@P18: branch of the decision tree, and ask, “Is the drug a small molecule?” and so on, until enough information
L827@P18: has been collected about the drug-program to make a prediction about its success or failure. By identifying
L828@P18: subtle patterns in a large enough database of historical drug transitions, machine-learning algorithms can
L829@P18: often make remarkably accurate predictions. Additional details on these techniques can be found in the
L830@P18: Methodology section.

L833@P19: Feature Importance
L834@P19: Given the stakes involved in the drug development process, machine learning forecasts must not only be
L835@P19: accurate, but also interpretable to stakeholders charged with the responsibility of making go/no-go decisions.
L836@P19: These decision-makers need to understand why a given forecast differs from the historical average, and
L837@P19: which features were most important in driving the difference or delta with respect to the disease-group
L838@P19: baseline LOA. To increase the transparency of our forecasts, the QLS machine learning algorithm reports the
L839@P19: most important features and their individual contributions to the forecast’s delta.
L840@P19: As an illustration, we decompose our probability of approval (POA) prediction of an oncology drug that
L841@P19: is currently in phase 3 clinical trials into its key components. (Note that we use the term POA for the QLS
L842@P19: machine-learning forecasts to differentiate from the empirical “LOA” presented in Part I.) Figure 14 reports
L843@P19: the top five features that increase this program’s estimated probability of approval from its therapeutic
L844@P19: area historical baseline of 35.0% from phase 3 to approval, to 71.8%. The top positive feature driving this
L845@P19: higher than average probability is its breakthrough therapy designation (+20.6%). This designation is granted
L846@P19: when the FDA has determined that preliminary clinical evidence indicates that the drug may demonstrate
L847@P19: substantial clinical improvement over available therapies. Further positive clinical evidence in phase 2
L848@P19: increases the estimate by an additional +6.4%. Next, because it is a PARP inhibitor (+4.6%) that has been
L849@P19: previously approved for another indication (+3.6%), the POA is augmented by an additional 8.2%. Finally, its
L850@P19: treatment of a solid tumor type (-2.0%) and the net aggregate contribution of other features (-2.8%) penalize
