L1157@P25: Discussion
L1158@P25: Comparison with previous reports on attrition rates
L1159@P25: In 2016, BIO published a major report on industry attrition rates using Biomedtracker’s data from 2006 to
L1160@P25: 2015 for 9,985 phase transitions, revealing an overall LOA of 9.6% for an asset entering Phase I development.
L1161@P25: This report analyzed 12,728 transitions from 2011 to 2020 and revealed a lower overall LOA of 7.9%. While
L1162@P25: the underlying data and analyses are consistent, we caution against direct comparison between the two topline LOA numbers.
L1163@P25: Among the additional ~2,750 phase transitions in this analysis, a sizable portion can be attributed to industry
L1164@P25: expansion, with more companies (1,779 vs 1,103) and a larger pipeline (9,704 programs vs 7,455) in the
L1165@P25: dataset. Additionally, increased demand from investors for disclosures on drug pipeline progress has resulted
L1166@P25: in timelier reporting of suspended programs. Furthermore, the time period examined in this report includes
L1167@P25: more rigorous scrutiny of the Biomedtracker database. The result is a stronger account of failed transitions
L1168@P25: in the 2011–2020 analysis vs the 2006–2015 analysis. In particular, this has resulted in a notable correction
L1169@P25: in the calculation of success rates for the Phase I transition, where drugs can lie dormant or unreported for
L1170@P25: many years, lowering the success rate from 63.2% during 2006–2015 to 52.0% in 2011–2020. Transition rates
L1171@P25: at Phase II and III are closer, albeit slightly lower in this update (28.9% vs 30.7%; 57.8% vs 58.1%). Regulatory
L1172@P25: approval moved in the other direction, increasing from 85.3% to 90.6%

L1202@P26: Oncology: largest segment of industry, modest success rates
L1203@P26: Oncology continues to possess the largest portion of the 12,728 phase transitions over 2011–2020, even
L1204@P26: though it has the lowest Phase I LOA (5.3%). Despite our caution against direct comparisons with the 2016
L1205@P26: paper, Oncology was one of the few major disease areas to increase its LOA, growing marginally from 5.1% in
L1206@P26: the previous analysis. In addition to the emergence of IO therapeutics, other drivers of improved success may
L1207@P26: be due to an increase in biomarker-defined patient populations, and the success of novel drug modalities
L1208@P26: (CAR-T, ADCs, etc.). That being said, the overall performance of Oncology is still considerably below the
L1209@P26: industry average (7.9%) and non-oncology indications (9.3%). There is also no substantial benefit to many
L1210@P26: Oncology indications being classified as rare diseases, as the rare Oncology subset only carries a Phase I LOA
L1211@P26: of 6.8%. These findings indicate that scientific complexity and the competitive dynamic that characterize this
L1212@P26: therapy area continue to have an adverse impact on the success rates for oncology therapies. This has not
L1213@P26: dissuaded investment within Oncology, with nearly half of all venture capital going into cancer companies by
L1214@P26: the end of the last decade.4
L1215@P26: Challenges for Phase II proof-of-concept drive overall performance
L1216@P26: The Phase II transition is clearly the main translational and limiting step from bench to bedside, as proof-ofconcept is only established in 28.9% of drug programs at this stage. Phase II also sees the greatest distribution
L1217@P26: between major disease areas, with a three-fold difference spanning Urology (15.0%) and Hematology (48.1%)
L1218@P26: at opposite ends of the spectrum. Within these disease areas, it is the performance of “novel” drug classes –
L1219@P26: NMEs (New Molecular Entities), biologics, and vaccines – that have the most significant negative contribution
L1220@P26: to low overall LOA success rates, as the “off-patent” grouping relies on duplication of established science.
L1221@P26: Hematology retains a dominant position largely on the strength of hemophilia research, which accounts for
L1222@P26: the largest number of transitions within the disease area. In the case of hemophilia A, where the underlying
L1223@P26: biology is extremely well characterized, 32 of 34 of phase transitions have been successful, including all six
L1224@P26: at Phase II development. The other main hematologic disease, anemia (which includes several different subindications), also has notably above-average success rates.
L1225@P26: Conversely, Cardiovascular saw a large numbers of failed Phase II transitions for novel drug candidates, with
L1226@P26: an overall rate of just 21.0%. Compared to the other major disease areas such as Oncology and Autoimmune,
L1227@P26: there is relatively little penetration of biologics within Cardiovascular, where novel drug discovery is commonly
L1228@P26: centered on oral small molecules. Of the 507 Cardiovascular development programs in the Biomedtracker
L1229@P26: dataset, there are just 98 biologics vs 321 NMEs. This approximate 1:3 ratio is far lower than Autoimmune
L1230@P26: therapies which have a ratio of 1:1 and a LOA of 10.7%. As noted in Figure 9, NMEs underperform biologics
L1231@P26: considerably, including at the critical Phase II transition. For biologics, there is typically a stronger relationship
L1232@P26: between disease state and drug mechanism, enabling a stronger proof-of-concept.
L1233@P26: Neurology is an interesting example where the Phase II transition rate (26.8%) is relatively close to the
L1234@P26: industry average, with most of the developmental risk realized later in Phase III studies. Clinical development
L1235@P26: programs for neurodegenerative diseases are often able (or designed) to find adequate justification from
L1236@P26: biomarker data to progress into late-stage trials, without having a clinical proof-of-concept on clinically
L1237@P26: validated endpoints. The resulting effect is underperformance in the Phase III and NDA/BLA transitions,
L1238@P26: dragging down the overall Phase I to LOA success rate for this disease category to 5.9%
L1239@P26: Characteristics of rare disease R&D not found in highly prevalent chronic disease
L1240@P26: Throughout the last decade, industry investment and drug development have pivoted towards rare,
L1241@P26: congenital diseases. Specific examples of clinical and commercial successes have encouraged this transition.
L1242@P26: Drivers of these successes include targeting molecularly defined causes of disease, regulatory incentives,
L1243@P26: and favorable reimbursement environments. However, as these drivers of success are absent from chronic,
L1244@P26: highly prevalent diseases, investment for these indications has waned over the same period, with notable

L1246@P27: large company exits in psychiatric and cardiovascular medicine. BIO has covered the state of innovation in
L1247@P27: these areas in a series of industry analysis reports.6,7,8
L1248@P27: One large difference between this 2011–2020 dataset and the previous 2006–2015 iteration is the
L1249@P27: intensifying focus on rare diseases. Our latest analysis includes 1,256 phase transitions within rare diseases,
L1250@P27: a considerable increase over the 521 noted in the previous study. This spans 685 different lead developers
L1251@P27: (not including those listed solely as partners). This indicates that companies view pivoting to rare disease
L1252@P27: clinical development as a sound strategy.
L1253@P27: While notably more successful than industry averages – and in particular chronic, highly prevalent diseases
L1254@P27: – the increasing breadth of rare disease R&D is leading to a reduction in overall success. Our calculated
L1255@P27: Phase I LOA of 17.0%, slightly over double the industry average (7.9%), represents a notable fall from the
L1256@P27: 25.3% reported in 2016. As the number of programs has grown, so has the number of rare diseases that
L1257@P27: are being addressed for the first time by therapeutic modalities where less is known about their biology and
L1258@P27: pathology. In other cases, there are new difficulties in targeting the root cause. Nevertheless, rare disease
L1259@P27: R&D remains an attractive area for investment, combining a large unmet need with commercial potential,
L1260@P27: supported by regulatory incentives and above-average clinical success rates.
L1261@P27: Complexity of modality correlates with higher LOA
L1262@P27: As an approximate rule, the increasing chemical complexity of the therapeutic being tested is associated
L1263@P27: with higher rates of phase success, as shown in Figure 10. In the past ten years the proportion of complex
L1264@P27: biologics in the clinical pipeline has steadily expanded from 25% to 40%.9 However, this trend has not been
L1265@P27: enough to boost overall industry rates beyond a 10% LOA, as simpler small molecules remain the in the
L1266@P27: majority. These account for 57% of the 12,728 phase transitions included in this analysis, with a modest 7.5%
L1267@P27: Phase I LOA.
