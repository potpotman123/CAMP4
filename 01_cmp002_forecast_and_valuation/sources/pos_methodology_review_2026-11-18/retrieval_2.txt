L79: ## SUMMARY
L80: Previous estimates of drug development success rates rely on relatively small samples from databases curated by the pharmaceutical industry and are subject to potential selection biases. Using a sample of 406 038 entries of clinical trial data for over 21 143 compounds from January 1, 2000 to October 31, 2015, we estimate aggregate clinical trial success rates and durations.
L81: We also compute disaggregated estimates across several trial features including disease type, clinical phase, industry or academic sponsor, biomarker presence, lead indication status, and time. In several cases, our results differ significantly in detail from widely cited statistics. For example, oncology has a 3.4% success rate in our sample vs. 5.1% in prior studies. However, after declining to 1.7% in 2012, this rate has improved to 2.5% and 8.3% in 2014 and 2015, respectively.
L82: In addition, trials that use biomarkers in patient-selection have higher overall success probabilities than trials without biomarkers.
L83: Keywords: Clinical phase transition probabilities, Clinical trial statistics, Probabilities of success
L84: ## 1. Introduction
L85: 
L86: The probability of success (POS) of a clinical trial is critical for clinical researchers and biopharma investors to evaluate when making scientific and economic decisions. Prudent resource allocation relies on the accurate and timely assessment of risk. Without up-to-date estimates of the POS, however, investors may misjudge the risk and value of drug development, leading to lost opportunities for both investors and patients.
L87: One of the biggest challenges in estimating the success rate of clinical trials is access to accurate information on trial characteristics and outcomes. Gathering such data is expensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to error. Previous studies of success rates have been constrained by the data in several respects.
L88: 【19†Abrantes-Metz and others (2005)】 surveyed 2328 drugs using 3136 phase transitions (e.g., from Phase 1 to Phase 2 in the approval process), while 【20†DiMasi and others (2010)】 studied 1316 drugs from just 50 companies. In the landmark study of this area, 【21†Hay and others (2014)】 analyzed 7372 development paths of 4451 drugs using 5820 phase transitions.
L89: In two recent papers, 【22†Smietana and others (2016)】 computed statistics using 17 358 phase transitions for 9200 compounds, while 【23†Thomas and others (2016)】 used 9985 phase transitions for 7455 clinical drug development programs. In contrast, ClinicalTrials.gov, the clinical trial repository maintained by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), contains over 217 000 clinical trial entries submitted by various organizations as of July 1, 2016 (see 【24†www.clinicaltrials.gov†www.clinicaltrials.gov】).

L23@P2: Executive Summary
L24@P2: This is both an updated study of clinical drug development success rates from our 2016 report, and an
L25@P2: expansion into the drivers of success with the addition of machine learning modeling to analyze the predictive
L26@P2: factors contributing to drug development. A total of 12,728 clinical and regulatory phase transitions were
L27@P2: recorded and analyzed from 9,704 development programs over the last decade (2011–2020), across 1,779
L28@P2: companies in the Biomedtracker database. Phase transitions occur when a drug candidate advances into
L29@P2: the next phase of development or is suspended by the sponsor. By calculating the number of programs
L30@P2: progressing to the next phase vs the total number progressing and suspended, we assessed the success rate
L31@P2: at each of the four phases of development: Phase I, II, III, and regulatory filing. Having phase-by-phase data
L32@P2: in hand, we then compared groups of diseases, drug modalities, and other attributes to generate the most
L33@P2: comprehensive analysis yet of biopharmaceutical R&D success.
L34@P2: This work was made possible due to the years of clinical program monitoring and data entry by Informa
L35@P2: Pharma Intelligence’s Biomedtracker, which subsequently populates the purpose-built Probability of
L36@P2: Technical Success (PTS) tool, Pharmapremia. BIO has long worked with Biomedtracker to calculate success
L37@P2: rates based on this data. More recently, BIO and Biomedtracker teamed up with QLS Advisors, a team of
L38@P2: expert data scientists who apply machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) to clinical trials data
L39@P2: and drug properties to assess the features that contribute the most amount, positively or negatively, to the
L40@P2: probability of approval. The computational analyses of over 200 different drug, trial, indication, and sponsor
L41@P2: metrics that influence clinical success rates were applied in a predictive capacity to produce risk assessments
L42@P2: and evaluations of R&D assets with increased accuracy.
L43@P2: Key Takeaways
L44@P2: • The overall likelihood of approval (LOA) from Phase I for all developmental candidates over 2011–2020 was
L45@P2: 7.9%.
L46@P2: • Phase II development remains the largest hurdle in drug development, with just 28.9% of candidates
L47@P2: achieving this critical phase transition.
L48@P2: • Of the 14 major disease areas, Hematology therapies had the highest LOA from Phase I (23.9%), representing
L49@P2: a seven-fold increase over the least successful group, Urology (3.6%).
L50@P2: • Immuno-oncology therapies provide a rare pocket of success in oncology R&D with an overall LOA of 12.4%
L51@P2: vs 5.3% for all oncology approaches.
L52@P2: • Rare disease therapies were notably successful with an overall LOA of 17.0%.
L53@P2: • Chronic, high prevalence disease therapies were less successful with an overall LOA of 5.9%.
L54@P2: • Biological complexity in drug modalities generally leads to higher LOA, with CAR-T and RNA interference
L55@P2: achieving the highest LOAs of 17.3% and 13.5%, respectively.
L56@P2: • Development programs with trials employing patient preselection biomarkers have two-fold higher LOAs
L57@P2: (15.9%), driven by a Phase II success rate of nearly one-in-two.
L58@P2: • The top contributing factors toward phase success are disease indication, target, modality, and drug
L59@P2: novelty.
L60@P2: • On average, it takes 10.5 years for a Phase I asset to progress to regulatory approval. Disease areas with
L61@P2-3: above-average LOAs tend to have the shortest development timelines.Contents
